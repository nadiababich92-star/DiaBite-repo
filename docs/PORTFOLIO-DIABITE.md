TL;DR (three arrows):
1. 40.1 million Americans live with diabetes and 115.2 million adults have prediabetes (CDC, 2023 data). Every meal asks the same question, "does this fit my day?", and tracking apps cost minutes per meal, so people give them up.
2. Build the quick check for the moment before eating: an agent understands the sentence, a deterministic engine computes the numbers, and a verifier blocks any number no tool returned. The model never produces a nutrition number.
3. It is live: 204 of 204 behavioural checks passed on the live agent on 21 October 2026, with a median answer time of 4.2 seconds. Next: five moderated sessions and a two-week beta.

01 Problem — "What's broken today, and for whom."
1. The question comes up at every meal, and a food list cannot answer it: the effect belongs to the whole plate, not to one food.
2. Calorie apps can answer, at a few minutes of work per meal. Sensor-based apps put a glycemic score behind a sensor that costs over $100 a month (from my competitor review). Nobody shows the arithmetic.
3. A diabetes educator told me many of his patients give up tracking after a couple of months. The effort is the cost.

02 Users — "Who we're building for and how they behave today."
Primary: adults in the US with type 2 diabetes, prediabetes or insulin resistance.
Behaviour today: they guess, skip the meal, or use a tracker for a while and stop.
Two invented people shaped the story: Maya, who has no time to plan and eats on the move, and Daniel, who plans every week and whose plan breaks.
Constraint: they are busy and not clinicians; a wrong answer matters to their health, so trust is the product.

03 Goals — "What success looks like for the user, and for the business."
A verdict in seconds, in plain words, before the meal.
The working shown on demand: every food, portion, glycemic index and load.
Never a wrong dose, never a number a model made up.
Measured trust: verified answers, tests that run on every change, and an honest list of what is not yet proven.

04 Key decisions — "The choices that shape the product, and the reasoning behind each one."
DECISION 1 — No number is ever generated by a model. Why: a model that writes a number cannot be audited. The model reads the sentence; an engine does the arithmetic from a food database; a verifier rejects any answer that contains a number no tool returned.
DECISION 2 — Safety lives in rules, not in prompts. Why: a refusal that depends on a model behaving is not a refusal. Dosing, red-flag symptoms, prolonged fasting and referral are caught by plain pattern matching before any model runs. A security audit found 9 of 21 dangerous phrasings slipping past (insulin by brand name, numbers spelled out, readings in mmol/L). All nine were closed in three days, and every phrasing that ever got through is now a permanent test: 72 of 72 pass.
DECISION 3 — Say what is unknown. Why: two slices of pepperoni pizza once came back with a glycemic load of zero. A food with no glycemic index is now never called "fits"; a food the database lacks is named and left out; a prepared dish it lacks is estimated from typical ingredients and labelled as an estimate.
DECISION 4 — A quick check first, a diary second. Why: a diabetes educator's feedback was that tracking is abandoned. The product's job is the decision before the meal; the diary exists for the days a plan breaks.
DECISION 5 — Three small agents instead of one. Why: different questions need different rules. A router sends each question to a meal specialist that can call the engine's tools, or to an advisor with no tools and no numbers. Cutting a meal turn from four tool calls to two took the 90th-percentile answer time from 26 seconds to under 9.

05 Solution — "How it was built, sequenced to prove the unknowns first."
1. The promise: an engine over 8,633 food records (USDA data, published glycemic-index tables, 2,000 recipes) and a verifier.
2. The agents and the safety gate on Azure AI Foundry: a router, a meal specialist and an advisor, with the rules in front of all three.
3. The product: Ask, Diary, a weekly Menu and Profile; email sign-in; a landing page; a dark "tape" look with cream slips; an accessibility pass.
4. The proof: behavioural checks on the live agent after every change, a security review (row-level security tested with two accounts, headers graded A+), and the measured cost per question.
5. Next: five moderated sessions with people who live with the condition, a two-week beta with 10 to 20 people, and conversations with dietitians about whether they would point patients to it.

06 Risks & mitigations — table
- A wrong number harms someone | Rules before any model, a verifier, no doses ever, a reference-tool banner, and the plain statement that no clinician has signed it off yet. I am asking dietitians whether it would help their patients.
- People abandon trackers | The product is a quick check, not a log. The beta measures return: 10 people logging on at least 4 of 14 days.
- Foods the database does not hold | About one typed phrase in eight in browser sessions. The product names what it does not know; every miss becomes a test.
- Data licensing | The glycemic-index tables have their own licences; permission is requested before any commercial use. Until then it is a demonstration.
- Model vendor and cost | The numbers come from a deterministic core, so the models can be swapped; a question costs about €0.003.
- Privacy | Only an email address, the person's answers about themselves and their diary, stored in Ireland, exportable and deletable.

07 Metrics — "How we'd know it's working: leading and lagging signals."
Behavioural checks on the live agent: 204 of 204 (21 October 2026)
Answer time: median 4.2 s, 90th percentile 6.4 s (102 cases)
Verified answers: 99% to 100% of turns
Cost per question: about €0.003
Planned beta gates: 90% of the beta's own phrases understood; 98% of live turns verified; under 20 seconds to log; 10 people logging on 4 of 14 days
Sessions: at least 3 of 5 would use it again without being asked

08 Reflections — "What I'd take from this into the next AI product."
Evals are the product. I run about a hundred cases against the live agent after every change.
Safety cannot depend on a model behaving. Put it in code that runs before the model.
The quiet bugs matter most. A zero where there should have been a number wrote my rule for unknown foods.
"I don't have that food" is a feature, not a failure.
Positioning mattered more than features: a quick check and a tracker are different products, and the second one is the one people stop using.
